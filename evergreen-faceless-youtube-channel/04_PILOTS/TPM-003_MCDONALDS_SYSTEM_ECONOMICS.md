| One-sentence thesis | McDonald’s is primarily a franchisor, but its conventional franchise model couples independently operated restaurants with a company-managed operating system, including site evaluation and a property/occupancy role that reinforces standardization and long-term incentives. |

## C. Narration Script

### 0:00–0:25 — Cold open

Walk into a McDonald’s in Tokyo, Tulsa, or Toronto and you will recognize the system before you recognize the person who owns the restaurant.

That is the strange achievement of franchising at scale. The operator behind the counter can be independent. The menu adapts by market. But the signs, operating rhythms, quality standards, technology, and customer expectations still feel like they belong to one machine.

So what does McDonald’s actually own behind the golden arches?

### 0:25–1:10 — Promise and stakes

The lazy version of the answer is, “McDonald’s is a real-estate company.” It is catchy. It is also too blunt to explain the business properly.

McDonald’s is primarily a franchisor. At the end of 2025, it reported **45,356 restaurants**, with approximately **95% franchised**. [1] That means the central question is not whether franchisees matter. They do. The question is how a company can let local operators run restaurants while still building one of the most recognizable operating systems in the world.

The answer sits at the intersection of brand standards, location, property and occupancy arrangements, training, menu systems, and a long-term relationship with franchisees. The restaurant is the visible product. The system behind it is the business.

### 1:10–3:05 — A franchise is not one thing

First, a clarification. “Franchised” does not mean every McDonald’s location is structured the same way.

McDonald’s says its franchised restaurants operate through conventional franchise, developmental license, or affiliate arrangements. The best structure can vary by restaurant, market, local regulations, property conditions, and the availability of qualified operators. [1]

The conventional franchise is the part of the model most relevant to the familiar real-estate story. Under that arrangement, McDonald’s generally owns or secures a long-term lease on the land and building. The franchisee pays for equipment, signs, seating, and décor. [1]

That does not mean every restaurant is identical or that the company owns every property beneath every sign. It means that, in a core form of the relationship, McDonald’s has a direct connection to the site itself while the franchisee brings capital, local operating responsibility, and entrepreneurial attention to the restaurant.

This division of labor is powerful because a restaurant is not just a kitchen. It is a location, a traffic pattern, a drive-thru geometry, a staffing system, a supply chain connection, a brand promise, and a set of standards that has to feel consistent enough for customers to know what they are getting.

### 3:05–5:10 — Location is part of the operating system

McDonald’s own franchising overview describes the site-selection process plainly. The company says it evaluates the site, acquires the property, and constructs the building after deciding a location should be developed. Franchisee selection is a separate process. [2]

That tells us something important. The business is not merely selling a name to someone who found a building. The system starts before the restaurant opens.

A good location is not a detail. It affects traffic, convenience, visibility, delivery access, parking, drive-thru flow, local labor, and the probability that a customer thinks “that will do” at exactly the moment hunger becomes a time-management problem.

If the company can build a repeatable method for evaluating locations, it can reduce variability before the restaurant has sold a single burger. And if it keeps a role in the property or occupancy structure under a conventional franchise arrangement, it has an additional connection between the physical site and the operating model.

That is why it is more accurate to call McDonald’s a system company with a meaningful site and occupancy component than to reduce it to a one-line real-estate slogan.

### 5:10–7:30 — The franchisee relationship turns standards into incentives

Why not simply operate every restaurant directly?

Direct operation offers control, but it also places more capital, management, staffing, and local execution risk inside the company. Franchising allows independent operators to own and run the restaurant business while using the brand, systems, and support of the network.

McDonald’s says its franchise relationships are fundamental to performance and brand protection. It also says that direct operation of some restaurants helps it test innovations, improve operating standards, and train personnel. [1]

In a conventional franchise, the company says franchisees primarily contribute revenue through rent and royalties based on a percentage of sales, with specified minimum rent payments, along with initial fees. [1] Those terms create a relationship tied to the health of the restaurant. The company needs the system to work. The franchisee needs the restaurant to work. The customer needs the experience to work well enough that the next visit does not become a different brand’s victory.

No incentive system is perfect. Franchisees can have different local priorities. The company may want a change implemented faster than operators prefer. Costs rise. Consumers change. But the design is trying to align a local business owner’s attention with a global operator’s standards and scale.

### 7:30–9:55 — Standardization is the invisible product

A McDonald’s restaurant sells food, but the deeper product is predictability.

Predictability is not glamorous. It is the result of thousands of decisions that become invisible once they work: operating procedures, training, food safety, supplier requirements, restaurant layout, technology, equipment, menu workflows, brand standards, and customer expectations.

McDonald’s says its conventional franchise/license agreements generally run for **20 years**. [1] That length matters because a restaurant system requires long-term decisions. A site must be developed. The building must function. Equipment and technology need updates. The operator needs a reason to invest. The brand needs a way to protect consistency without attempting to supervise every individual judgment from a distant office.

This is where franchising becomes a network model rather than a collection of licensing transactions. The brand gives the customer a reason to enter. The operator makes the local business work. The company’s systems make the experience repeatable. And the property/occupancy structure helps tie the physical location to the larger operating architecture in conventional arrangements.

### 9:55–11:40 — The model has limits

The system is powerful precisely because it is structured. That also makes it demanding.

A franchisee is not a passive investor. McDonald’s says it generally does not work with passive investors and requires rigorous standards from franchisees. [1] The company’s success still depends on local execution, labor, customer experience, supply conditions, regulation, and competition. A location can be well selected and still face a difficult market. A standard can be well designed and still be poorly executed.

And there is no universal arrangement. In developmental license and affiliate structures, licensees may provide capital and the real-estate interest, while McDonald’s receives royalties and fees. [1] That is why the simplistic claim that “McDonald’s owns all the real estate” fails. It turns a varied global business into a bumper sticker.

The useful lesson is more interesting: control can be distributed. A company does not need to personally operate every unit to build a durable system. But it needs to know which parts of the system must remain coherent—brand, standards, training, site strategy, incentives, and the economics that connect them.

### 11:40–13:10 — Resolution

So what does McDonald’s own behind the sign?

In its conventional franchise model, it generally owns or secures a long-term lease on the land and building, while franchisees invest in other parts of the restaurant and operate the business. [1] But the more important asset is the coordinated system: the way the site is selected, the brand is protected, the restaurant is run, and the incentives are designed over a long relationship.

At year-end 2025, McDonald’s had more than **45,000 restaurants** across its system. [1] You do not create that kind of recognizability by merely licensing a logo. You create it by deciding which parts of the customer experience must be standardized, which parts can be operated locally, and how both sides are rewarded for making the restaurant work.

The transferable lesson is that scale is not always about centralizing every task. Sometimes it is about holding onto the right control points and making the rest of the network stronger because those points are reliable.

### 13:10–13:35 — Next-view bridge

McDonald’s uses a network of operators and sites to turn a restaurant into a system. Next, we will go inside self-checkout, where a machine changes the economics of labor, loss, speed, and customer patience—often all at once.
