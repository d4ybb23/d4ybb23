## C. Narration Script

### 0:00–0:25 — Cold open

Costco sells enormous packs of things people did not know they needed in enormous packs. But the strange part is not the pallet of paper towels. It is that one of the world’s biggest retailers has built its identity around keeping prices so low that the merchandise margin is deliberately modest.

In fiscal 2025, Costco reported nearly **$270 billion** in net sales. Yet the number that explains the business may be the **$5.323 billion** it collected in membership fees. [1] The fee is not an add-on at the door. It is one of the levers that lets the entire store behave differently.

### 0:25–1:15 — Promise and stakes

So why does Costco make money by selling so little—at least compared with a conventional big-box retailer? The answer is not that every product is cheap. And it is not simply that membership fees are “pure profit,” a phrase that is more dramatic than it is precise.

The real answer is a system. A membership changes the customer relationship. A limited assortment changes purchasing and labor. Fast inventory turnover changes cash flow. A warehouse changes the physical cost of retail. Put those pieces together, and the price on the shelf becomes both a sales tool and a credibility test.

By the end of this episode, you will be able to see Costco less as a store with unusually large mustard and more as a machine designed to convert trust, volume, and repetition into an operating advantage.

### 1:15–3:20 — The model begins with a constraint

Most retailers try to win by offering more choice. More brands, more sizes, more colors, more aisles in which to compare three nearly identical things until the afternoon quietly disappears.

Costco begins by rejecting much of that complexity. Its 2024 annual report says the company aims to offer low prices on a **limited selection** of national-brand and private-label products. The idea is not to be everything to everyone. It is to choose fast-selling products, buy in volume, move them quickly, and remove cost from the journey between supplier and shopper. [2]

That matters because choice is expensive. Every additional item has to be selected, forecast, purchased, shelved, counted, replenished, discounted when it misbehaves, and eventually explained to someone who would like to know why there are six versions of the same shampoo.

Costco said it carries **fewer than 4,000 active SKUs** in a core warehouse. That is a radically narrow assortment by broadline-retail standards. [2] Fewer items mean more attention per item. More attention can mean larger purchase orders. Larger orders can strengthen purchasing power. And when a store concentrates demand into a smaller set of products, those products can move through the building faster.

This is the first part of the model: Costco is not trying to create the most complete store. It is trying to create a highly productive one.

### 3:20–5:45 — The warehouse is a cost decision

The second part of the model is hiding in plain sight: the building looks like a warehouse because it is meant to function like one.

Costco describes its facilities as no-frills, self-service warehouses. Merchandise can remain on pallets or racks rather than being individually staged like a department-store display. The company says its floor plans are designed for economy in selling space, handling, and inventory control. [2]

That aesthetic has an economic purpose. If the customer is willing to do a little more of the work—navigate the large space, accept less theatrical presentation, carry bulk items, sometimes assemble a cart that appears ready for a small expedition—the retailer can spend less on some of the labor and display costs that make conventional retail polished but expensive.

Costco also uses a distribution approach built for throughput. Its filing says merchandise often moves from suppliers to cross-docking depots and then quickly to warehouses. Rather than storing goods through a long chain of handling steps, the company uses those depots to consolidate freight and create handling efficiencies. [2]

The key word is not “cheap.” It is **simple**. A simpler operating design can lower costs. Lower costs make low prices more believable. And believable low prices make the membership feel worth renewing.

### 5:45–8:20 — Membership changes the relationship

Now we get to the annual fee.

In a normal store, a retailer has to persuade you to walk in today. At Costco, the relationship begins before the first item goes into the cart. A member has already made a small commitment. That does not guarantee a visit, of course. But it changes the psychology of each visit. Once the fee is paid, the member has an incentive to make the membership feel useful.

And Costco has an incentive to make that decision feel smart, repeatedly. The promise is not simply low prices in the abstract. It is the feeling that, if you look carefully, the store is usually giving you a deal worth belonging to.

That is why membership economics are more subtle than a simple arithmetic trick. In fiscal 2025, Costco reported **$5.323 billion** in membership fees. [1] That is a real, material revenue stream. But the bigger strategic effect is that the fee encourages Costco to protect the value proposition that made someone join in the first place.

The company’s 2024 filing reported a **92.9%** renewal rate in the United States and Canada and **90.5%** worldwide. [2] Those figures are reported for that period, and future renewal rates can change. But as a business-system signal, they show why trust is central. A retailer can raise prices or create friction once. A membership business has to persuade people to return to the relationship every year.

Costco’s Executive membership adds another layer. The 2024 filing said Executive members accounted for about **73.3% of worldwide net sales**. [2] That does not mean the membership tier alone created those sales. It does show that the relationship is tied to the highest-spending portion of the customer base.

So the fee is not merely revenue. It is a governance mechanism. It forces Costco to defend a promise: if the prices stop feeling sharp, the assortment gets sloppy, or the experience loses credibility, the renewal decision becomes a vote.

### 8:20–10:50 — Volume, turnover, and cash

The next piece is inventory.

Retail is full of businesses that buy inventory, hope it sells, mark it down when it does not, and then discover that their cash has become emotionally attached to a warehouse full of seasonal items.

Costco’s model attempts to make inventory move quickly. The company says its high sales volume and rapid turnover combine with volume purchasing, efficient distribution, and reduced handling to support lower gross margins than many other retailers. [2]

The company also notes that it often sells inventory before it has to pay for it, while taking advantage of early-payment discounts. [2] This is not a guarantee of a permanently easy cash cycle. It is an illustration of how fast turnover can improve a retailer’s position. When goods move quickly, capital is tied up for less time. When the store turns a small number of items over and over, the system does not need every product to carry a heroic margin.

That is why the business cannot be reduced to one line item. Membership fees, limited assortment, warehouse design, supplier relationships, cross-docking, turnover, and customer trust reinforce one another. Take away the low-price credibility and the membership loses force. Add thousands of slow-moving products and the operating simplicity erodes. Turn the warehouse into a boutique and the cost structure starts arguing with the promise.

### 10:50–12:35 — The trade-offs

This model comes with trade-offs, and those trade-offs are not bugs.

You do not get unlimited choice. You do not necessarily get the exact brand, size, or flavor you expected. You may buy more than you intended because the format makes small purchases feel mildly unpatriotic. And a warehouse club is not equally convenient for every household, location, or shopping mission.

Costco also operates in a fiercely competitive retail landscape. Its own filing names global, national, and regional competitors across supermarkets, supercenters, online retailers, gasoline stations, discounters, and other warehouse clubs. [2] Low prices are not a moat by themselves. Other businesses can cut prices. The more difficult thing to copy is the collection of operating choices that makes those prices sustainable enough to keep earning trust.

This is the distinction that matters. A discount can be a tactic. A cost system, a membership relationship, and a high-turnover operating model can become a business architecture.

### 12:35–14:00 — Resolution

So, why does Costco actually make money by selling so little?

Because the model does not ask every transaction to do all the work. The membership fee helps fund a relationship. The relationship gives Costco a reason to protect price credibility. The limited assortment concentrates volume. The warehouse format strips out some retail costs. Fast turnover makes inventory and cash work harder. And the result is a store that can look almost austere while operating at extraordinary scale.

At the end of fiscal 2025, Costco operated **914 warehouses**. [1] That scale matters. But scale is the outcome of a system that first had to become believable at a single warehouse, with a single member asking a very simple question: was this worth the fee?

The transferable lesson is not “start a membership club.” It is this: sometimes the strongest business model is the one that makes the customer relationship—not the individual sale—the center of the machine.
