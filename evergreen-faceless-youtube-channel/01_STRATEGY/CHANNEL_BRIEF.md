# Channel Brief — The Profit Map

**Document owner:** Channel Principal  
**Decision status:** Approved working direction; validate legal/name availability before public use  
**Revision cadence:** Quarterly, or after 12 published long-form episodes

## 1. The One-Sentence Position

> **The Profit Map reveals the hidden business logic behind the companies, products, and systems people use every day.**

The channel is a faceless business-documentary property, not a finance-advice channel, market-news outlet, motivational brand, or generic “top ten companies” factory. Each episode should isolate one counterintuitive question, explain the mechanism with source-backed storytelling, and leave the viewer with one transferable mental model.

| Brand element | Locked decision | Why it is right for this business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Working name | **The Profit Map** | Broad enough for company, industry, and product stories; specific enough to imply a mechanism. |
| Tagline | **The hidden business logic behind the things you use.** | Clear promise with everyday relevance. |
| Primary format | 12–18 minute narrated business-system documentary | Sustains a substantive argument while remaining producible weekly. |
| Release rhythm | Weekly after a three-episode buffer | Optimizes for quality and compounding library value over raw upload volume. |
| Audience language | English | Broad initial market and coherent production standard. |
| Revenue priority | Long-form advertising revenue; sponsorship/affiliate only after trust is established | Keeps editorial incentives aligned with evergreen viewer satisfaction. |
| Editorial temperature | Curious, precise, lightly cinematic, never breathless | Gives a faceless channel a recognizable personality without faking drama. |

## 2. Audience Contract

The target viewer is a practical, curious adult who enjoys the satisfaction of seeing a familiar thing become legible: why the cheap hot dog matters, why a flat box can change a global supply chain, why a franchise system is not merely a licensing agreement. They may work in business, technology, operations, sales, management, or simply possess a healthy suspicion that the free shipping is not actually free.

| Audience job | Channel response | Proof inside the episode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Make a familiar business make sense.” | Begin with a visible contradiction and resolve it. | The opening is answerable in one sentence but earns its depth. |
| “Give me intelligence, not jargon.” | Translate finance and operations into plain-language causal chains. | Every term is defined by use, not by lecture. |
| “Do not waste my time.” | Deliver the title promise inside the first 75 seconds. | The viewer knows the thesis, stakes, and route of the story early. |
| “Show me how you know.” | Label graphs, date numbers, retain sources, and distinguish fact from inference. | The dossier supports every material claim. |
| “Give me a reason to watch another.” | End with a specific adjacent mechanism, not a generic plea. | End screen and pinned comment point to a natural next episode. |

## 3. Editorial Pillars and Allocation

The pillars create variety without confusing the audience or recommendation system. The first 30 videos should remain within these boundaries; new categories need a written rationale, not a passing enthusiasm for a spreadsheet with unusually good cheekbones.

| Pillar | Allocation across first 30 | The core viewer question | Example subjects |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Brand moats | 30% | Why is this company hard to compete with? | Costco, IKEA, LEGO, McDonald’s, Netflix. |
| Everyday economics | 25% | Why does this ordinary product or service work this way? | Popcorn, free shipping, gym memberships, airline overbooking. |
| Industry machines | 20% | What hidden system makes this industry run? | Warehouses, data centers, containers, last-mile delivery. |
| Strategic failures and reinventions | 15% | Which decision broke—or saved—this business? | BlackBerry, Borders, LEGO, Toys “R” Us. |
| Business tools and work systems | 10% | What mechanism changes how work gets done? | Network effects, subscription economics, procurement, enterprise software. |

## 4. Content Inclusion and Exclusion Rules

| Eligible subject | Why it belongs | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A company with a documented strategic mechanism | It offers a concrete story and evidence base. | Avoid a profile with no causal question. |
| An everyday product with a hidden economic model | Familiarity makes the mechanism clickable. | Do not overstate margins or present an anecdote as an industry law. |
| An industry infrastructure system | Maps, diagrams, and flows create original visual explanation. | Secure enough credible data to avoid hand-waving. |
| A historic business failure or reinvention | Conflict gives narrative propulsion. | Explain multiple causes; do not reduce an enterprise to one smug moral. |

| Excluded subject | Why it is excluded at launch |
| --- | --- |
| Stock picks, “buy now” calls, or personalized financial recommendations | High trust and compliance burden; misaligned with the documentary promise. |
| Daily earnings or market news | Short shelf life undermines the evergreen library strategy. |
| Celebrity net-worth gossip and outrage | Weakens audience quality and encourages packaging over substance. |
| Generic AI-voice “company facts” compilations | Risks repetitiveness, reused-content concerns, and an absence of a genuine editorial thesis. [1] |
| Unlicensed clip compilations | Rights risk and weak visual transformation. |

## 5. Narrative Grammar

Every episode uses the same underlying grammar but must not feel templated. A repeatable episode is not a repetitive episode; the mechanism changes, the visual metaphor changes, and the viewer should feel an actual editor was present in the room.

| Beat | Purpose | Minimum proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The contradiction | Make the business puzzle impossible to ignore. | A specific, defensible tension—not bait. |
| The promise | State what will be explained and why it matters. | The title is fulfilled in the opening. |
| The world before | Establish the market, customer, or operational constraint. | Context that materially changes the interpretation. |
| The mechanism | Explain how money, attention, inventory, or incentives move. | At least one original flow, map, or diagram. |
| The trade-off | Explain the cost, risk, failure mode, or competitor response. | Counterargument or constraint is treated fairly. |
| The resolution | Return to the opening question and generalize the mental model. | One clear “therefore” statement. |
| The next bridge | Point to the closest adjacent mechanism. | A named video or existing playlist. |

## 6. Operating Economics and Success Definition

This is a catalog business. The first-year objective is not a promised revenue number or a theatrically rapid monetization date. It is a coherent library that earns durable search, suggested, and browse opportunities while holding a clear standard for original research and visual explanation. Full YPP advertising eligibility is currently subject to threshold and review requirements; reaching a threshold does not guarantee acceptance. [2]

| Horizon | What success actually means | Not a success metric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First 90 days | Eight to twelve strong episodes or a credible pipeline toward them; usable learning on hooks, packaging, and retention. | “Did a random upload go viral?” |
| Months 4–6 | A repeatable production bottleneck is identified and one adjacent topic cluster emerges. | Publishing more weak episodes solely to hit volume. |
| Months 7–12 | A recognizable catalog, growing session pathways, clean rights records, and a sustainable cadence. | Pretending average internet CPM figures are revenue forecasts. |
| Mature channel | Multiple evergreen entry points and a documented editorial advantage. | Dependence on one personality, trend, sponsor, or source of traffic. |

## 7. Brand Voice

Write like an informed friend who has been invited to the warehouse back room and has brought a flashlight, a calculator, and enough manners not to call the CEO a supervillain. Prefer active verbs, concrete nouns, short sentences for key turns, and lightly cinematic contrast. Avoid pseudo-profound openers, faux urgency, moral grandstanding, unqualified predictions, and the phrase “this changes everything” unless the shipping container has actually just changed the price of nearly everything.
